Why it usually goes wrong

The problem was never the tool.

Almost everyone gets the same three disappointments in their first month. None of them are fixed by a better model, and all of them are teachable in an afternoon.

01

It sounds right and is wrong

Confident paragraphs, invented citations, a number that shifted by 20%. You can’t tell which sentence to check, so you either trust all of it or stop using it.

The fix: the three-bucket split and a ninety-second verification ritual that catches almost every real failure.

02

You don’t know what to ask

“Help me with my report” returns something generic, so you rewrite it four times and give up. The tool wasn’t the problem: the request was one line of a six-line brief.

The fix: the six-line brief, plus a reusable context block you paste once and keep forever.

03

It never touches your actual work

Chat is great at thinking and useless at doing. Your files, your spreadsheet, your assignment brief — none of it is there, so the answer is always about someone else’s project.

The fix: knowing which surface does what, and how to point an agent at a real folder without handing it the keys to everything.

01

What these models actually are

Foundations · 7 minutes · one drill

A model doesn’t look things up. It predicts what a good answer would look like, from patterns in training data. That makes it extraordinary at shape — structure, tone, code, phrasing, the form of an argument — and unreliable at specifics: numbers, names, dates, links, quotes. It also has no memory between conversations, and a finite working memory that degrades as it fills.

In plain words

“The model isn’t looking anything up. It’s writing the most likely-sounding answer, which is why the shape is always good and the details are sometimes invented.”

If that felt fast — read it slower

Read the first sentence again and stop there. It writes well because it learned what good writing looks like. It gets facts wrong for the same reason — it’s guessing what usually comes next, not checking. So: use it for structure, tone and drafts. Verify numbers, names, dates and quotes yourself. That’s the entire lesson.

Do this

  • Ask for something specific you can check: a statistic, a citation, a date. Then verify it. Do this once and you’ll never blindly trust again.
  • Send the same prompt twice in two fresh chats and compare. The variance is the point — it’s sampling, not a database.
  • Ask it to separate what it knows from your input, what it’s inferring, and what it would be guessing at.

Before you answer, split your knowledge into three buckets:
1. What I told you
2. What you can infer from it
3. What you would be guessing at
Then answer, and mark anything from bucket 3 inline as [guess].

Straight answer

Who this is for, and who it isn’t.

It’s for you if…

  • You’re a student — business, design, engineering, anything — and you want to use AI without it becoming an academic-integrity problem.
  • You’ve tried the tool, got something impressive and something wrong, and can’t tell the difference next time.
  • You learn better when things are explained twice, slowly, with an example.
  • You want to actually build something — a page, a dashboard, a 3D scene — not just chat.
  • You’re changing careers, or running a small business and doing everything yourself.

Skip it if…

  • You want a list of magic prompts and nothing else.
  • You want the AI to do your assignment while you watch. The course is explicitly against that, in lesson 24.
  • You’re a machine-learning engineer looking for model internals. This is about working with agents, not training them.
  • You need an accredited qualification. You get a printable completion card, honestly labelled as that.

Questions

The things people ask before starting.

Do I need to know how to code?

No. Levels 1–3 assume nothing. Level 4 gets you building pages and 3D scenes by describing them and reviewing the result — you never write code by hand, but you do learn to read enough of it to tell whether it’s right.

Which AI tool do I need?

Any capable one. The examples use Freebuff because that’s what I built it in, but every lesson is about briefs, context, verification and judgement — things that transfer to Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Cursor or whatever ships next month.

How long does it take?

About 13 hours in total if you do everything: roughly 7 hours of core lessons, 2½ hours of deeper reading and worked examples, 2½ hours of lesson tests, and 1 hour of level checkpoints. That does not include the drills you do on your own work, because those depend on you. Most people do 20 to 30 minutes a day for six to eight weeks. There are no deadlines and nothing expires — the course waits for you.

Is this just a list of prompts?

No. Prompts are the toolkit at the end. The course is 38 lessons, each with deeper reading, a drill you do on your own work, and a three-question test with an explanation for every answer. After each level there is a ten-question checkpoint, mostly to prove you still have it a week later. If you only want prompts, this will feel like too much reading.

I’m not a fast reader. Will I keep up?

That’s who it was rewritten for. Every lesson has an In plain words summary in two or three sentences, and an If that felt fast version that repeats it slower with an example. Comfort reading mode increases the type size and opens every slow section at once.

How do I get in?

You sign in with Google, GitHub or Apple through Freebuff, and the course opens on that account — free right now, no payment step. No password to invent, nothing to remember. Signing in is how I know who has worked through it, which matters later when it goes on sale.

Do you see what I do inside the course?

No. Progress, notes and your name are stored in your own browser and never leave it. The server records only that you signed in and when you opened the course — which is how the Classroom tier can tell you who has actually started.

Is there a certificate?

A printable completion card with your name, your rank and the date, generated when you finish. It is not an accredited qualification and it doesn’t pretend to be one.

Can I use it with a class or a group?

Yes — it's free right now, so just send them the link and have each person sign in. When it goes on sale, the Study group and Classroom tiers cover five and thirty separate accounts, and I add those addresses to the access list by hand.

What if I get stuck?

Every lesson has a “check yourself” question and the slower explanation. If you’re still stuck, email me with the lesson number and what you tried — I answer, and confused questions are how the next edition gets better.
